# What Makes A Good Question?

"Questioning matters" is certainly true, but if we stop there, it says almost nothing. What really needs to be advanced is another layer: what kind of question counts as a good one?

We should first clear away a few common misunderstandings. A good question is not the same as a grand one, a novel one, an abstract one, or one that leaves people unable to answer. A question can be very small and still be a good question. It can also be very grand and still be only rhetorical emptiness. The value of a question does not depend on how deep it sounds; it depends on whether it truly organizes the unknown into a structure that can move forward.

So a good question is, first of all, not a sentence that merely looks like a question. It is a constrained unknown.

At minimum it should contain several basic elements: what object it is about, which quantity is actually missing, what the known conditions are, which variations are ruled out, what kinds of moves are allowed to advance it, and what counts as answering correctly. Only when these things have been made clear to some extent does the question stop being just a topic of discussion and begin to have the meaning of solvability.

Seen from this angle, a good question has at least five features.

First, it distinguishes among different answers. That is, different answers must make a real difference, rather than all being able to count as correct with enough generosity. If a question accommodates every answer, it demands nothing at all.

Second, it excludes irrelevant degrees of freedom. A good question does not leave every direction equally plausible. It cuts away large amounts of irrelevant variation and forces attention toward the truly crucial gap. It does not open the space; it tightens it.

Third, it exposes the truly missing variable. Many times we think we "do not know many things," but a more precise description is that we do not know which missing piece is most worth knowing. The role of a good question is not to display the breadth of ignorance, but to locate the bottleneck of ignorance.

Fourth, it leads to further advance. That is, it should make subsequent decomposition, verification, recursion, refutation, comparison, or experimental design possible. If a question cannot generate any next operation, then at most it is an exclamation, not a problem.

Fifth, it allows the answer to be substituted back. Once an answer is produced, it should be possible to place it back into the question, rather than merely creating the effect of "it seems explained" somewhere nearby. If it cannot be substituted back, then the question and the answer were never really connected.

Conversely, bad questions also have several typical forms. Some are too diffuse: everything can be discussed, so nothing becomes clear. Some are too empty: they have no criterion, and can only elicit attitude expression. Some are too mixed: they bundle together several different layers of problem into one sentence and appear deep while being impossible to advance. Others look like questions grammatically while failing, cognitively, to establish any real relation between unknown and constraint.

So a workable definition is:

> A good question is one that, under a given goal, uses the least but sufficient structure to expose the real unknown as a gap that can be advanced.

In this definition, "least" means not preserving irrelevant information together with the rest. "Sufficient" means not deleting the structure that truly affects the solution. "Advanceable" means that the question must drive subsequent operations rather than merely produce an aura of grandness.

This lets us see more clearly that the value of a question does not lie in sounding like a philosophical sentence, but in whether it effectively compresses degrees of freedom. A good question is not one that asks big, but one that cuts accurately. It does not mystify the unknown; it locates it.

If this understanding is right, then the next step is unavoidable: what exactly does "advanceable" mean? How far must a question be able to advance before it really has a solvable structure? At that point, questioning stops being merely a linguistic act and starts to approach an algorithmic way of organizing thought.
